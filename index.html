Must-Try Foods & Drinks
Brunost waffles
Heart-shaped Norwegian waffles with brown cheese (brunost) and jam. Uniquely Norwegian, unlike anything else. Have this Day 1 at any café.
Fiskesuppe
Norwegian fish soup — creamy, packed with fresh fish and shellfish. Every fish restaurant has a version. Fiskeriet's is among the best.
Gravlaks
Cured salmon — better here than anywhere. Dill-cured, silky, rich. Available as a starter almost everywhere.
Game stew
Slow-cooked reindeer or elk with mashed potatoes and lingonberries. The definitive Norwegian comfort dish. Den Glade Gris does this exceptionally well.
Rømmegrøt
Sour cream porridge with butter, sugar, and cinnamon. An ancient Norwegian staple that sounds unremarkable and tastes extraordinary.
Local cider
You'll taste it first in Sogndal. The fjord's sheltered microclimate has grown apples since the medieval period. Dry and complex — ask for it at any bar.
Aquavit
Norway's national spirit — caraway or dill-flavored, oak-aged, served ice-cold. Worth trying at least once. Linie is the most famous Norwegian label.
Torsk (cod)
Norwegian cod at its finest. Norwegians have been fishing it for a thousand years and still prepare it better than anyone.

History & Regional Context

You'll be moving between two very different historical worlds — Oslo, a capital forged by plague, fire, and Norse power struggles, and the Sognefjord interior, where Viking sea battles and medieval stave churches sit against the deepest fjord on Earth.

OsloWhere Norway's power was forged
Before 1000 AD — Origins
The area's old name, Ánslo, is interpreted as either "meadow at the foot of a hill" or "meadow consecrated to the Gods." A modest Viking-era trading hub at the tip of the Oslofjord. Three Viking ships have been excavated nearby — now at the Viking Ship Museum.
1048–1299 — Viking trading post to capital
Founded as a trading place around 1049 by King Harald Hardrada. In 1299, King Haakon V moved permanently to Oslo, making it the de facto capital, and began construction of Akershus Fortress to defend it from Swedish invasion.
1349–1624 — Plague, Danish rule & fire
The Black Death arrived in 1349 and killed roughly half the population. The Kalmar Union of 1380 united Norway under Danish rule — Copenhagen became the real capital and Oslo was reduced to a provincial town for 400 years. In 1624, a fire burned for three days and destroyed most of the city. King Christian IV rebuilt it in stone beside Akershus Fortress, renaming it Christiania.
1814–1925 — Independence & a name reclaimed
Norway broke from Denmark in 1814. The city (still called Christiania, later Kristiania) functioned as capital through a union with Sweden until 1905. In 1925, it officially reclaimed its medieval Norse name: Oslo.
20th century to now
Nazi Germany occupied Norway in 1940 for five years. After liberation in 1945, Oslo hosted the Winter Olympics in 1952. The Opera House opened in 2008 and launched a transformation of the eastern waterfront. Current population: roughly 724,000.

Sogndalsfjøra & the SognefjordKing of the Fjords
Geology — How the fjord was made
The Sognefjord is the longest (205 km) and deepest (1,308 m) fjord in Norway, carved by glaciers nearly 3,000 m thick during the last glaciation. The inner end of the fjord sits directly below Jostedalsbreen, continental Europe's largest glacier. The near-vertical walls you'll ride alongside are the direct scar left by that ice.
Viking Age — The Battle of Fimreite (1184)
In 1184, the decisive Battle of Fimreite was fought on these waters — King Sverre defeated and killed King Magnus Erlingsson, consolidating royal power across Norway. You'll pass the site on the shuttle ride in from Sogndal on Day 1.
Medieval — Stave churches & Christianity
The Urnes Stave Church — which you visit on Tour Day 3 — was built around 1130 and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Built without nails using post-and-beam techniques descended from Viking shipbuilding traditions. The carved doorway panels define the "Urnes style" of Norse art.
Agriculture & microclimate
The inner Sognefjord benefits from a sheltered microclimate — warmer and drier than the coast because surrounding 2,000-metre peaks block Atlantic weather. The apple orchards you'll ride through have been producing fruit since the medieval period. The region now has a growing cider scene.
Language note
Western Norway, including Sogndal, uses Nynorsk as its official written language — reconstructed from western rural dialects in the 19th century, quite different from the Danish-influenced Bokmål used in Oslo. You'll notice the shift in signage as you travel west.

History tied to your daily rides

▸
Day 3 — Urnes Stave Church (c. 1130) One of the oldest wooden buildings in the world still in its original location. The carved doorway panels blend Christian imagery with Viking animal interlace patterns.
▸
Day 4 — Lustrafjord & WWII The Sognefjord's deep water provided cover for U-boats during the German occupation. Solvorn is a beautifully preserved 18th–19th century wooden village.
▸
Day 5 — Nigardsbreen Glacier The glacier reached maximum extent around 1750 (Little Ice Age) and has been retreating since. The 1750 terminal moraine is visible on the hike in.
The Danish footprint — connecting both regions Both Oslo and the Sognefjord were under Danish control for roughly 400 years (1380–1814). This is why Oslo was renamed Christiania, why Lutheran churches replaced medieval Catholic ones, and why Bergen — not inland Sogndal — became the dominant commercial hub of the west. The Sognefjord region remained agrarian and Norse in character — that continuity is part of why the stave churches, apple orchards, and Nynorsk language survived here.
Birds to Watch For

July is peak summer in the Sognefjord — birds are nesting, active, and highly visible. The mix of deep fjord water, high mountain passes, farmland, glacial streams, and old-growth forest creates remarkable habitat diversity within short distances.

White-tailed Eagle
Haliaeetus albicilla
Norway's most iconic raptor — wingspan up to 8 feet, known locally as havørn ("sea eagle"). Often seen soaring above fjord cliffs or scanning the water for fish. Watch from the RIB boat on Day 4.
Fjord cliffs · RIB boat · Day 4
Common Dipper
Cinclus cinclus
The only songbird that swims underwater. Plump, dark brown with a bright white chest. Walks into fast-moving streams against the current. Near-certainty on the Fortun Valley ride — look at any rocky stream crossing.
Mountain streams · Day 1
Eurasian Nutcracker
Nucifraga caryocatactes
Striking crow-family bird, chocolate-brown with dense white spotting. Lives in conifer forests at elevation. Famous for caching thousands of pine seeds and remembering nearly every location. Watch below Sognefjellet on Day 2.
Conifer forest · Day 2
Snow Bunting
Plectrophenax nivalis
Males in summer breeding plumage are strikingly black and white. Found on snowfields and rocky tundra at high elevation — exactly the Sognefjellet summit terrain. Often gather on bare patches near the road.
Sognefjellet summit · Day 2
Bluethroat
Luscinia svecica
One of Norway's most beautiful small birds — males have a vivid blue throat with a rufous-red spot at its center. Common in the Sognefjord region in summer. Listen for its rapid, complex song near willows or stream edges.
Fjord margins · valley floors
Grey Heron
Ardea cinerea
Tall, patient, silver-grey wading bird. Very common along fjord shorelines — often standing motionless in shallow water. Visible from the bike on virtually any day near the water.
Fjord shoreline · all days
Common Sandpiper
Actitis hypoleucos
Small, bobbing wader ubiquitous on rocky lakeshores in summer. Has a characteristic "teetering" motion — constantly bobbing its rear end. Hafslo Lake (Day 4) and Nigardsbreen lake margins (Day 5) are prime spots.
Lake edges · Days 4 & 5
Fieldfare
Turdus pilaris
Large, handsome thrush with a grey head, chestnut back, and spotted breast. Extremely common in farmland and orchards in summer — you'll hear the loud chuckling "chak-chak-chak" call constantly throughout the tour.
Farmland · orchards · all days
Also watch for: Northern Wheatear (rocky alpine areas, very common on Sognefjellet), Rock Ptarmigan (grey-brown grouse at elevation on Day 2), Red-throated Diver (glacial and fjord lakes), Eurasian Oystercatcher (bright orange bill, fjord edges), Common Eider (large sea duck — males striking black and white). From the RIB boat on Day 4, harbour porpoises are occasionally spotted in the Lustrafjord.
